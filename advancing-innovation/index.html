Focus Group Findings Advancing Innovation

DateDecember 2026

Methods

1 focus group
5 participants
60 minute session
Who took part
3 staff in Educational or Student Services 1 staff in Business Services 1 staff in ACCESS

Participants voluntarily signed up to take part in the focus group sessions when they completed a Google Form at the September General staff meeting. The Google Form asked them which, if any, focus groups they would like to participate in, largely centered around the different areas of the 5-3-1 Initiative. Based on the focus groups respondents stated they would like to participate in, they were then placed in at least one of those topic areas. After placement, participants then decided if they would still like to participate in the focus group to which they had been assigned.

Step two

Focus groups were conducted, recorded and transcribed.

Step three

Joseph analyzed the focus group transcripts for major themes, takeaways and recommendations.

Identifying Gatekeepers

Participants discussed how identifying gatekeepers in a system is a critical component when translating a program or intervention to another context. Importantly, participants discussed that gatekeepers exist at all levels such as a Superintendent, Director and even teachers that are defacto leaders for their content area or grade level. Identifying these people and getting their buy-in to a project before implementation is critical.

In their words
I think you're right that [a gatekeeper] could be anyone, but you need people with power to be able to champion it
I think there is something to be said about the key players and the people who come in that can bridge that gap, because to your point, you have risk management who's saying no, you can't do this, right? And you need your data points to then sell them on that. So if you don't have those key players that come in to help, then make this palatable for all of your people, you kind of won't end up anywhere. You get stonewalled at one place or another continuously.

Getting Belief from All Stakeholders

In addition to identifying gatekeepers, focus group participants discussed the importance of having belief in the program or project from all stakeholder groups, but in particular the belief of teachers. Those in the focus group differentiated between the idea of buy-in and belief. They saw buy-in as a commoditiy that we could acquire, but belief as something that outlasts a funding initiative and goes deeper. Those in the focus described that ensuring staff have voice and agency when implementing programs is key to getting their belief.s

In their words
You have to make it something that the kids can be excited about and the teachers can be invested in, and if it's really difficult for them to adjust to something, if it's added to their day, if it's something extra that they have to do, I don't see them being invested in it, no matter how innovative it is, and if the kids, if if it doesn't stick on the kid level
So like I think [NAME]'s example was perfect because yeah you crushed it on the student involvement [piece] like anything in our world where you want that traction, [you need support] from staff students families

Teachers Are Critical

While focus group participants discussed the importance of many stakeholder groups, they consistently kept coming back to the importance of teacher belief of a program for its success. Not only did participants identify teachers as the ones ultimately implementing a program, but they also identified teachers as the ones who help to drive much of school culture. Therefore, the implementation of a program and its successful transfer to another context lies heavily on the influence of teachers.

In their words
And I think like with both what [NAME] is saying is like it has to be easy and it has to be meaningful, and both the staff and the students have to be invested
But I mean, you know, this is just if if you don't have a teacher that's going to say that's a great idea and I'm going to do it, and and you might have just one. But that's really all it takes because if one teacher wants to do it, then that might light the spark.
I think it would die there if you don't have a passionate teacher that's going to want to champion it

Other Recommendations

This sub-theme is comprised of other one off recommendations from focus group participants. One recommendation is to consider how success of spread and scale is defined, the other is to setup previous grantee mentors for future cohorts.

In their words
And I think it's important, lastly, to say, how are you defining success? So if you're saying success is every district in every you know 10 schools in a district are using it, you might not get to 10. But if you reshape your idea of success and say, what if five? What if seven teachers? Is that not innovative? Is that not you know? Because I feel like sometimes we just say it's got to be this big massive thing, and it'd be great if it was. But oftentimes, if you reach a few, that's still success, you know.
I'm hearing from you, [NAME] that you could like make the original awardee like a mentor for the next one, where it's like here's a guide functionally who's done this already to help you work through the the trials that you want to do.

Providing Reflective Roadmaps

Focus group participants stated that having previous awardees create detailed roadmaps to recount their experiences, detailing what they planned on doing and how they were able to stick to that plan or how they had to deviate. Focus group participants felt that providing these "reflective roadmaps" could be an essential part of future implementation and to ensure that the project feels doable for future teams who make take it on. 

In their words
You could even do something as structured like I think of like the payroll, like the business side, where it's like, here's how we paid for the people. Here were the things we did to enact the the fiscal portion. Like we got the money, but then dispersing the money is this whole other challenge, right? And so even something like that, where it's less about implementation, right? Because implementation will be so context dependent, but here are the pragmatics
Step one is it could be this. Step two looks like that. Here's what we did, and then it got done. You know, because people often like, oh, that sounds like a daunting task. How would where would we even start? And if there's a thing that says like, well, here's how we did it, and here's the 32 things that we had to do. At least they might it might fill some gaps of fear 

Creating Defined Roles

Focus group participants found that when projects were successful in multiple contexts, one contributing factor to success was the definition of clearly delineated roles for different team members. Participants described the stage of, "who is doing what" could sometimes be an awkward and prolonged stage during the implementation of a project and this stage would stop projects from really gaining the momentum they might have otherwise. 

In their words
We had like a team that had completed the model all the way through [progress]. They were our [example]. So it's like that structure is what comes to mind when you're saying scaling...It wasn't like an entire district, but it was 12 very specific roles within your district that you had to commit to. So you had to have like a decision maker, you know, like a like a director or superintendent level for us, and then you had to have like a coordinator level with me, a student, a parent, community partner, teachers, administrator.

Ensuring a Champion is Charismatic

Participants described that a single project may have multiple "champions", but the primary face of the project needs to be someone who is charismatic and is able to speak to many different stakeholder groups. Participants mentioned that how a project is pitched to a superintendent versus a teacher versus a student are all very different and if there is a primary champion of a project, they should be able to speak to all of those audiences. 

In their words
I think a follow up would be find somebody in that space, and I hate to be so like esthetic wise, but somebody who's charismatic and is good at hyping up people with the idea because you need that initially. You got to get people that are like, I want to do that, but it can't just be to your point a slideshow of somebody saying this is what we did and here's the data that shows how impactful it was for the land and for the people
So the superintendent can have this brilliant idea, and he could think it's a wonderful thing, but if you don't have someone to speak to that teacher, it won't work

Live Experiences

Participants strongly felt that the best way to showcase the innovative projects was to do it live so that other educators, community partners and business leaders could experience them. Participants emphasized that the program as one that is, "funding innovative ideas" should represent similar innovation in its convenings, materials and other live events. 

In their words
It'd be cool to have this in a conference center that is not just, I mean, not boring adult. It'd be like some very cool, innovative place, you know. So it's like an experience. It's an immersive experience with these ideas that people feel like, 'oh, I can get behind that, and I have got help and support'
If we're going to create transformative learning experiences for students, shouldn't we be creating transformative learning experiences for our organization.
I also think in like replicating those experiences for adults. Like we're asking everyone to be innovative, but think about your K 12 experience, your college experience. We didn't do any of these things, so why are we the experts on that? And I was talking with my group. Like my number one pet peeve is when I go to like a training for something and it's supposed to be innovative, and they're reading PowerPoint slides to me. Like what a wasted opportunity not to recreate that learning experience for me. Yeah, so they can show me, and I can internalize it, and I can be inspired, and then I can adjust and adapt for my relevant audiences.

Bringing Funders to Educators

Focus group participants felt a role OCDE staff could play is bringing in local business, corporations and community partners to educators so that educators with innovative ideas could potentially have their ideas funded at an OCDE event. Participants additionally considered the idea of utilizing the Bright Futures Foundation to act as a resource to acquire the funds or at least coordinate with local partners. 

In their words
[What if] they received offer a day like a a conference whereby they share and showcase and with students there if possible what they accomplished and invite people you know in a summit and a summit and just say and here's and if you're interested here's how you replicate it transparently here's how much it cost, and then maybe OCDE, if they have more money from DA or whatnot, or maybe this Bright Futures Foundation could say, "You like that idea? You have the capacity to do it. You just don't have the money now. Bright Futures can give you that money"
But you know, we we know a lot of sponsors that have worked with different other events that we've done, so you can get sponsors to because I think what's one of the ideas that one of these people have done?

Teacher Voice and Belief

Participants once again emphasized that having teacher voice, agency and belief as part of the spread and scale process is crucial. At one point in the focus group, participants seemed to agree on the idea that teachers trust other teachers more than anyone else and if a prospective district was able to hear from a teacher who has implemented the project already, that would go a long way in a future district choosing to implement the innovative project. 

In their words
[An example program] kind of switched it to like teachers or staff buy in to like believe in it. I think you really have to believe in it. If you're not required to do something in addition to. It has to come from within, like that. And not that that's the best answer because it's like okay, how do you get them to believe in? 
It's the believing in it, and I think in order to believe in it, you have to give [teachers] a voice at the table.
[Teachers] need a practical way of doing it, so people need to have a way that it doesn't feel overwhelming, that it feels palatable, it feels realistic
[Facilitator] Do you think teachers trust other teachers more than anyone else in like a school educational system?

[Participant] Oh, 100%. Yeah, and...for better for worse, they are the most critical audience that you could possibly have because they've seen it all.

Everyone's Biggest Recommendation

Each focus group participant gave their most important takeaway or recommendation from the discussion.

Participant #1 emphasized that a showcase or convening that is innovative and interesting was their strongest recommendation to consider

I really like the showcase idea. Yeah, I think that if you have something that's working at a school that they're very proud of, that they want to continue on with, showcasing that to the rest of the district or to other schools that are Interested in having something like that program in their school or in their district, I think that's a that would be a great way to showcase it. I just really do think that there's power in in kids talking about their excitement in something and teachers being involved and talking about it

Participants #2 & 3 both emphasized that teacher belief was the most critical piece to successful spread and scale

Get the teachers who are excited and passionate about it, and then give them the the platform to just you know make it make it known that like these people are passionate about it, and then...once that spark goes, [other teachers are] gonna see it, whether it's in the newsroom or whether it's in some other way, it's like, oh, that teacher is really excited about it.

Participant #4 emphasized that a charismatic champion was their biggest takeaway

You need a human at the forefront to hit all the levels that can talk to a superintendent, that can talk to the board of directors, that can make the pieces make sense for each and every audience that can go to the students, can go to the teachers, and tell their story of what this is and how it's going to be great. And then six months down the road, follow up and say this is why it was great. So you need that person to be able to champion it because you can have the greatest structure in the world, but your implementation will go way off if you don't have those key people in place to help sell it to all of the levels that need to be believing in it.

Participant #5 added on to #4's biggest takeaway by stating a teacher would be a good choice for the "face" of a project

And I would really make sure that...if you have the choice between having a director or a teacher be the proponent of the idea, go with the teacher.
